Abstract

Token-level steering has emerged as a pivotal ap-
proach for inference-time alignment, enabling
fine-grained control over large language models
(LLMs) by modulating their output distributions
without parameter updates. While effective, exist-
ing methods rely on dense intervention at every
decoding step. This persistent manipulation not
only incurs substantial computational overhead
but also risks compromising generation quality
by excessively drifting from the model’s intrinsic
distribution. In this work, we show that dense
intervention is unnecessary and propose Sparse
Inference-time Alignment (SIA), which per-
forms sparse junction steering, intervening only
at critical decision points along the generation
trajectory. Our key insight is that high-entropy
junctions mark pivotal decision points in the gen-
eration trajectory and are particularly susceptible
to misalignment, indicating the need to introduce
alignment-related reward signals at these points.
Extensive experiments across different model fam-
ilies and alignment objectives show that steering
only 20%–80% of tokens achieves the superior
alignment–efficiency trade-offs. For strong base
models such as Qwen3, intervening on as few as
20% of tokens matches or even surpasses heavily
post-trained instruct models. This sparsity en-
ables stronger guidance while better preserving
the model’s native distribution, integrates seam-
lessly with search-based methods (e.g., Best-of-
N), and reduces computational cost by up to 6×.

2. Background

Existing inference-time alignment methods can be broadly
categorized into three paradigms: prompt-based, search-
based, and refine-based approaches, depending on the stage
when alignment is enforced during generation.

Prompt-based Alignment (Lin et al., 2024; Bai et al.,
2022b; Alkhamissi et al., 2024). These methods steer LLMs
at the pre-generation stage by encoding task instructions
or behavioral constraints directly into the prompt, thereby
biasing the model’s initial generation distribution. Although
simple and computationally efficient, their effectiveness is
limited by the expressiveness of natural language prompts
and the model’s ability to reliably follow them, particularly
for complex or fine-grained alignment objectives.

Search-based Alignment (Khanov et al., 2024; Zhou et al.,
2024; Stiennon et al., 2020). These methods intervene at
the in-generation stage by leveraging external preference
signals to guide decoding as it unfolds. Conceptually, they
perform guided search over the model’s generation space,
favoring trajectories that better satisfy alignment objectives.

Based on the granularity of guidance, these methods can
be further categorized into token-level, chunk-level, and
sentence-level approaches. This work focuses on token-
level steering, which offers the finest control and the greatest
potential for precise alignment. Most existing token-level
methods primarily focus on improving preference signal
quality and apply guidance uniformly across all tokens.
Such dense steering ignores the sparsity of critical deci-
sion points, often leading to unnecessary computational
overhead and an imbalanced trade-off between the model’s

intrinsic distribution and external rewards.

Refine-based Alignment (Ji et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025;
Yuan et al., 2025). These methods operate at the post-
generation stage. Their core idea is to iteratively improve an
initially generated, potentially misaligned response through
multiple rounds of refinement, ultimately yielding a more
aligned output. Representative approaches such as Aligner
(Ji et al., 2024) and DIFFPO (Chen et al., 2025) perform
refinement directly in the token space, whereas methods
like SEA (Yuan et al., 2025) intervene in the logits space to
adjust the generation distribution. While effective, refine-
based methods typically require additional inference rounds,
leading to increased latency. As such, they address align-
ment at the response level, rather than correcting misaligned
decisions during the generation process itself.

Discussion. Notably, these three paradigms are complemen-
tary rather than mutually exclusive. Their integration across
different generation stages can leverage synergistic effects,
potentially leading to superior alignment performance.

3. Preliminaries

3.1. Token-level Markov Decision Process

To mathematically formalize the text generation process of
Large Language Models (LLMs), we formulate it as a token-
level Markov Decision Process (MDP), which is typically
denoted by a tuple M = {S, A, P, R}. The components of
this framework are defined as follows:

• State Space S: A state st ∈S represents the sequence
of tokens available at time step t.
It consists of the
initial prompt (or query) x = {x1, x2, . . . , xN} con-
catenated with the sequence of tokens generated so far
y<t = {y1, y2, . . . , yt−1}. Formally, the state is repre-
sented as st = [x, y<t].
• Action Space A: The action at at time step t corresponds
to selecting the next token yt from the model’s vocabulary
V. Thus, the action space A is essentially the vocabulary
V, i.e., at ∈V.
• Transition Function P: In the context of LLM genera-
tion, the transition between states is deterministic. Given
the current state st and the chosen action at, the next state
st+1 is formed by appending the action to the current
sequence: st+1 = P(st, at) = [st, at].
• Reward Function R: The reward function R : S × A →
R maps a state-action pair to a scalar value rt = R(st, at).
This function provides token-level feedback, which can
be derived from a learned reward model (as in RLHF) or
predefined control signals.

Under this MDP framework, an LLM is viewed as a token-
level policy π(at|st) that generates the next token by sam-

the generation process toward states with higher expected
future returns to achieve alignment with human preferences.

4. Sparse Junction Identification

We formalize sparse steering as a correction to the implicit
assumption of uniform intervention in inference-time align-
ment, by explicitly modeling when value guidance should
be applied during decoding. We first introduce a general
formulation for sparse steering, then describe the construc-
tion of a high-fidelity token-level value model that enables
reliable local credit assignment, and finally study gating
strategies for identifying alignment-critical junctions.

4.1. Formulation of Sparse Steering

The optimal policy π∗derived in Sec. 3.2 specifies how
value guidance can be incorporated into decoding. Impor-

tantly, it does not prescribe that such guidance must be
applied at every decoding step. In practice, the influence of
individual tokens on the final sequence reward is highly non-
uniform, with only a small subset of tokens acting as pivotal
decision points. To this end, we propose a sparse value
guidance strategy that identifies and guides those critical
tokens with a substantial impact on the alignment outcome.

Formally, we introduce a gating mechanism G, which deter-
mines whether to invoke the value function at time step t.
The modified sparse decoding policy is formulated as:

4.2. Value Model

To enable effective token-level value guidance, a reliable
value model V∗is essential. However, existing methods
suffer from a mismatch between trajectory-level rewards
and token-level decisions. ARGS (Khanov et al., 2024) ap-
plies trajectory reward models directly to partial sequences,
leading to noisy local signals due to insensitivity to interme-
diate states. In contrast, lookahead-based methods such as
Transfer Q∗(Chakraborty et al., 2024) rely on multi-token
simulation, which improves foresight but incurs high com-
putational cost and variance. To overcome these issues, we
adopt a distillation-based training paradigm (Mudgal et al.,
2024; Liu et al., 2025b) to align coarse trajectory rewards
with fine-grained token-level values.

4.2.1. TRAINING STRATEGIES

Our core insight is to treat the trajectory-level reward of
a full response as the global supervision signal for every
constituent token state. Formally, let x denote the prompt
and y = {y1, y2, . . . , yT } be the corresponding response of
length T. We utilize a pre-trained trajectory-level reward
model R(x, y) to provide a ground-truth score for the com-
plete sequence. We optimize our token-level value model
Vθ by enforcing that the average of predicted values across
the entire sequence aligns with the global reward. This ob-
jective encourages the model to learn a stable and consistent
value distribution throughout the training process. Given

Settings. Our training corpus is derived from WildGuard-

To evaluate the generalizability of our trained value models,

Skywork-Reward-V2 as the trajectory-level reward model,

Skywork-Reward-V2-Qwen3-1.7B for Qwen3-1.7B).

4.3. Gating Mechanism Design and Evaluation

In this section, we formally define the diverse gating mech-
anisms G investigated in this study. We categorize these
mechanisms based on different information dimensions and
subsequently evaluate their efficacy in identifying critical
junctions for value-guided alignment.

4.3.1. EXPLORING DIFFERENT GATING MECHANISMS

A gating mechanism determines if a guidance signal should
be applied at a given state st. We define the gating output
as a binary indicator gt ∈{0, 1}, where gt = 1 triggers the
value model guidance. We explore four primary dimensions:

Random Gating. As a stochastic baseline, we implement
a sparse guidance strategy by randomly selecting junctions.
For each token position t, given a threshold p ∈[0, 1], the
gate is defined as:

gt = I(zt ≤p),
zt ∼Uniform(0, 1),
(7)

where I(·) is the indicator function. This serves as a con-
trol group to evaluate the necessity of informed junction
identification.

Position-based Gating. Prior research (Qi et al., 2025a;
Song et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2025b) suggests that the initial
tokens in a response are disproportionately influential, as
they establish the semantic trajectory and tone for subse-
quent generation. Recent safety alignment study (Qi et al.,
2025a) also indicates that post-training shifts in distribution
are concentrated in early time steps. We define a positional
gate with a window size w:

gt = I(t ≤w).
(8)

This strategy focuses guidance on the foundational stages
of the decoding trajectory.

from alignment objectives, making external value guidance
particularly beneficial.

4.3.2. EMPIRICAL EVALUATION

Settings. To determine the optimal G, we adopt the exper-
imental configuration from Sec. 4.2.2, evaluating Qwen3-
1.7B and Llama-3.2-1B on the HEx-PHI and AlpacaEval
datasets with Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B as the
evaluator. Specifically, we conduct a parameter sweep over
the thresholds of various gating strategies to analyze their
performance across a range of steering ratios.

Results.
Figure 1 compares different gating strategies
across steering ratios, models, and alignment objectives.
Entropy-based (absolute entropy) gating consistently dom-
inates all alternatives, achieving higher performance ceil-
ings and stable improvements across nearly the entire inter-
vention range. In contrast, random gating underperforms
across settings, indicating that sparsity alone is insufficient
without informed junction identification. Position-based
and attention-based gating exhibit more constrained be-
havior. While position-based gating is competitive at low
intervention ratios on safety-oriented benchmarks, its per-
formance saturates quickly and degrades on helpfulness
tasks. Attention-based variants show similar limitations,
with gains less consistent and more sensitive to steering
intensity. These results indicate that structural or positional
heuristics capture only task-specific regularities and fail to
generalize across alignment objectives.

Taken together, these trends suggest that intrinsic model
uncertainty, rather than position or structural proxies, is
the primary signal associated with alignment-critical de-
cisions. We therefore adopt entropy-based gating as the
default mechanism in subsequent experiments. More results
can be found in Figure 5 in App. D.2.

5. Sparse Junction Steering

In this section, we conduct comprehensive experiments us-
ing the selected entropy-based gating strategy to demon-
strate the effectiveness and efficiency of SIA.

5.1. Experimental Settings

Models. To verify the generalizability of our findings, we
evaluate SIA on both Base (Pre-trained) and Instruct (Post-
trained) versions of the Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025) and
Llama-3 (Grattafiori et al., 2024) families. For standard
evaluations, we adopt a conventional setup where the value
model is of equivalent size to the corresponding LLM. This
setup is conducted on Qwen3 (0.6B, 1.7B, and 4B) and
Llama-3.2 (1B and 3B). Furthermore, to explore the Weak-
to-Strong (W2S) (Burns et al., 2024) scaling property, we
test SIA on larger-scale models by using smaller value mod-
els to guide stronger LLMs. Specifically, we employ a 4B
value model to guide Qwen3-8B and 14B, and a 3B value
model to guide Llama-3.1-8B.

Datasets. We consider the three primary alignment pillars
(3H) (Bai et al., 2022a): Harmlessness, Helpfulness, and
Honesty. Our evaluation covers the full sets of the following
benchmark datasets: HEx-PHI (Qi et al., 2024) for Harm-
lessness, AlpacaEval (Dubois et al., 2024) for Helpfulness,
and TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) for Honesty. Detailed
descriptions of these datasets are provided in App. C.2.

Baselines.
We evaluate SIA against both officially re-
leased, extensively post-trained Instruct models and multi-
granularity search-based methods. For Base models, we
evaluate alternative inference-time alignment strategies; for
Instruct models, we assess whether sparse steering can fur-
ther improve already post-trained systems. Specifically, we
include two analogous search-based baselines: sentence-
level Best-of-N (BoN) (Stiennon et al., 2020) and chunk-
level Chunk-based Search (CBS) (Zhou et al., 2024). To en-
sure a fair comparison, all search-based methods utilize the
same reward signals provided by our trained value models,
with hyperparameters calibrated to align their computational
overhead with that of full-token intervention (see App. C.3
for details).

Metrics. Following the settings in Sec. 4.2.2, we employ
the state-of-the-art trajectory-level reward model Skywork-
Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B (Liu et al., 2025a) as the auto-
mated judge to calculate reward scores and evaluate the
alignment performance of the generated responses.

Implementation Details. To maintain a focus on exploring
the sparsity of inference-time alignment rather than hyper-
parameter tuning, we adopt the default weighting factor
β = 1.0 from prior works (Khanov et al., 2024; Xu et al.,
2025). A detailed analysis of the impact of this parameter
is provided in Sec. 6.1. Furthermore, the training details of

the value model are elaborated in App. C.1.

5.2. Normal Setting

As shown in Figure 2, our comprehensive evaluation reveals
several consistent patterns across models and datasets.

1. Sparse steering is sufficient for optimal alignment. For
nearly all evaluated models, peak alignment performance
is achieved by steering only a sparse subset of tokens, with
the optimal steering ratio remaining between 20%-80%.
Increasing the steering ratio beyond this point does not lead
to further improvements and may even degrade alignment
quality, underscoring the importance of selective rather than
exhaustive steering.

2. The proposed method generalizes across model stages.
SIA consistently improves both Base and Instruct models,
with more pronounced relative gains observed for Base
models. This behavior is expected, as Instruct models have
already undergone extensive alignment during post-training.

3. SIA achieves superior efficiency compared to coarse-
grained baselines. Across most settings, steering approxi-
mately 20% of tokens is sufficient to match the performance
of BoN-8, representing a roughly 4× reduction in computa-
tional overhead. Meanwhile, a steering ratio in the range of
20%–40% achieves parity with CBS-8, resulting in a signif-
icant 2×–4× gain in efficiency. The detailed computational
overhead calculation and analysis are deferred to App. D.1.

4. Base models can rival Instruct performance with
sparse steering.
For the Qwen3 family, Base models
equipped with a sparse steering ratio of 20%–40% are able
to match, and in some cases exceed, the performance of
their corresponding Instruct counterparts on the majority of
benchmarks. This suggests that lightweight inference-time
steering can effectively replicate key benefits of large-scale
post-training for sufficiently capable pre-trained models.

5. Performance gains are bounded by base model ca-
pacity. For Llama-3 Base models, although SIA achieves
parity with Instruct versions on safety-related benchmarks,
a performance gap persists on helpfulness and honesty tasks.
Our analysis in App. E.1 indicates that this limitation stems
from the base model’s inherent deficiencies in text fluency.

We provide additional generation examples of SIA in the

6. Deep Analysis

In this section, we provide a more granular analysis of SIA
from various dimensions to offer deeper insights into its
underlying mechanisms. Unless otherwise specified, all
analytical experiments are conducted using Qwen3 (1.7B
and 4B) and Llama-3.2 (3B). The evaluation is based on
100 randomly sampled instances from the HEx-PHI and
AlpacaEval datasets.

6.1. A Sparse Perspective on LLM-Reward Trade-offs

Alignment in LLMs essentially entails a trade-off between
preserving the model’s intrinsic distribution and maximiz-
ing the external reward. While training-based methods (e.g.,

7


Inference-time Alignment via Sparse Junction Steering

RLHF) employ KL-divergence penalties to maintain proxim-
ity to the reference distribution, inference-time approaches
use a weighting factor β to control the strength of steer-
ing. Nevertheless, indiscriminately steering every token
during inference often results in sub-optimal outcomes. SIA
re-evaluates this trade-off through a novel perspective.

As shown in Figure 4, when the steering ratio is 100%, cor-
responding to traditional full-token guidance, increasing β
does not consistently improve performance. Instead, it can
lead to significant degradation, as observed with Qwen3-
1.7B and 4B on AlpacaEval. However, by employing sparse
steering, we find that we can achieve superior performance
at higher guidance intensities while simultaneously improv-
ing efficiency. This suggests that efficiency and performance
are not always a zero-sum trade-off. We argue that uniform
intervention is suboptimal, as it ignores the conflict between
the value model and the LLM’s inherent distribution, as
well as the noise in the value model signals (as theoretically
analyzed in App. B.4). Sparse guidance provides a new
dimension for this trade-off, allowing for a better equilib-
rium between the LLM’s original distribution and the target
distribution of the rewards.

6.2. Balancing Multi-Objective Alignment

Another central challenge in LLM alignment is the trade-off
between multiple, often conflicting, objectives (e.g., being
helpful vs. remaining harmless). We examine this problem
within the context of SIA. Given that the entropy threshold is
the primary hyperparameter in SIA, a natural question arises:
does the optimal threshold vary across different alignment
objectives? If so, multi-objective balancing would necessi-
tate task-specific threshold tuning. To investigate this, we
visualize the performance metrics under the normal settings
across various models and datasets, as shown in Figure 6 in
App. D.3. Our analysis reveals several key insights.

1. Consistency of Optimal Threshold. Interestingly, across
different model families and scales, the entropy threshold
that yields peak alignment performance remains remarkably
consistent, typically centered around a value of 1.0. De-
creasing this threshold to steer a larger fraction of tokens
does not lead to further performance gains and, in many
cases, results in a slight degradation of quality.

2. Variation in Intervention Density. The fundamental dif-
ference in aligning diverse objectives lies in the proportion
of steered tokens rather than the optimal threshold. Specif-
ically, safety alignment (Harmlessness) demands a higher
steering ratio than non-safety tasks (Helpfulness and Hon-
esty). This stems from the model’s internal distribution:
harmful prompts induce higher state entropy, reflecting in-
creased uncertainty. Consequently, entropy-based gating
identifies more critical junctions that require external steer-
ing to maintain a safe trajectory.

6.3. Hierarchical Search

Since SIA operates at the most granular token level, it can
be seamlessly integrated into other coarse-grained search
methodologies to establish a more efficient hierarchical
search framework. Figure 7 in App. D.4 shows the results
of integrating SIA with BoN sampling. Across various mod-
els and alignment objectives, the combination of BoN +
SIA consistently achieves significant improvements in both
inference efficiency and the peak alignment performance
attainable.

Our analysis shows that traditional BoN is inherently ineffi-
cient because it evaluates alignment only after generating
full sequences, making highly aligned trajectories unlikely
when misalignment accumulates across multiple divergent
nodes. In contrast, SIA intervenes early at high-entropy de-
cision points with targeted reward signals, effectively prun-
ing unpromising trajectories on the fly and concentrating
computation on more aligned paths, thereby achieving both
higher efficiency and better final alignment performance.

6.4. Sparse Entropy Reduction

To understand how SIA reshapes the output distribution,
we analyze the entropy change post-steering (Figure 8 in
App. D.5). Our findings are threefold.

1. Alignment as Entropy Reduction. Moderate sparse
steering lowers distribution entropy, guiding the model from
a disordered state toward an aligned one. Compared with
static post-training, this method is more controllable and
plug-and-play.

2. The Entropy Rebound. Increasing the intervention ratio
or strength does not yield monotonic entropy reduction. Be-
yond a critical threshold, excessive intervention overwrites
inherent low-entropy nodes with external noise, triggering
an entropy rebound and increasing uncertainty.

3. Saturation and Reward Hacking. Crucially, lower en-
tropy does not linearly translate to better alignment. While
entropy continues to drop as β increases from 0.5 to 4.0,
performance (see Figure 4) often saturates or degrades. This
discrepancy likely stems from noise in the value signals (see
App. B.4 for details) or from a distribution that has been
sharpened rather than semantically corrected.

7. Conclusion

In this paper, we present SIA, a highly effective and ef-
ficient alignment framework that performs steering exclu-
sively at sparse junctions during inference. Our approach is
grounded in the core insight that intervening at high-entropy
positions prevents the accumulation of minor deviations
that ultimately lead to alignment failure. Through com-
prehensive experiments across various model families and

generalizability. Our multi-dimensional analysis further
elucidates the underlying mechanics, establishing SIA as a
robust solution for high-impact model alignment.

A. Future Direction

More Precise and Efficient Gating Strategies. While SIA demonstrates the efficacy of a simple entropy-based gating
mechanism, it remains a relatively coarse heuristic for identifying alignment-critical junctions. Future work could explore
more sophisticated or diverse strategies, such as semantic entropy to better capture linguistic uncertainty, or multi-dimensional
gating criteria that combine structural and information-theoretic metrics. Developing such composite strategies could further
refine the precision of sparse interventions, maximizing performance while minimizing computational overhead.

High-Fidelity Value Models. Because SIA relies on sparse, targeted interventions, the sensitivity to the quality of guidance
signal is significantly amplified. To fully unlock the potential of sparse steering, it is crucial to design high-fidelity value
models specifically optimized for these critical junctions. Enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio of the guidance signal at
detected sparse nodes will be a key factor in achieving more robust alignment.

Diverse Guidance Mechanisms. The current implementation of SIA primarily focuses on logits modulation to steer the
model’s output. However, the identified sparse junctions represent deep structural transition points that may be amenable
to other forms of intervention. Future research should investigate more diverse guidance strategies, such as hidden state
activation steering (Kong et al., 2024) to provide more nuanced control over the model’s reasoning paths.

Scaling with Pre-trained LLMs. Our empirical results suggest a strong correlation between the capabilities of the base
model and the effectiveness of SIA. As pre-trained LLMs continue to evolve in instruction-following, we expect SIA to yield
even more significant gains, potentially surpassing traditional post-training alignment methods with even lower intervention
density. Testing the scalability of SIA across a wider range of frontier models is a priority.

Extension to Multi-Modality. The principles of sparse inference-time alignment are not inherently limited to LLMs. A
compelling next step is the extension of SIA to Vision Language Models (VLMs) and other multi-modal architectures.
Identifying alignment-critical junctions in cross-modal attention layers could provide a path toward efficient and controllable
multi-modal generation.

B. Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide a theoretical justification for the proposed sparse value guidance strategy. We analyze the trade-off
between computational efficiency and alignment performance, demonstrating that: (1) the performance degradation from
sparse decoding is upper-bounded by the cumulative criticality of skipped steps, and (2) in the presence of value estimation
noise, sparse steering can surpass dense steering by acting as a regularizer.

Divergence at step t as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the optimal policy and the base policy:

∆t := DKL(π∗(·|st)∥πbase(·|st)).

C. More Details

C.1. Value Model Training

Backbones. To ensure consistency across our evaluations, we train value models (VMs) that correspond to the scales of the
LLMs used in our experiments. Specifically, for the Qwen3 series (0.6B, 1.7B, and 4B), the VMs are initialized directly from
their respective pre-trained base models (e.g., VM-Qwen3-XB is initialized from Qwen3-XB-Base). For the Llama-3 series
(1B and 3B), we leverage the Reward Models from Skywork-Reward-V2 as initialization (e.g., VM-Llama-3.2-XB utilizes
Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.2-XB). Empirical results suggest that this initialization strategy significantly accelerates
convergence and enhances final performance. To adapt these backbones for value estimation, we append a linear reward
head to the final transformer layer, enabling the model to output a scalar score. Notably, the same value model is employed
for both the Base and Instruct versions of a given model architecture.

Datasets. Our training pipeline incorporates two primary datasets: WildGuardMix (Han et al., 2024) and UltraFeedback
(Cui et al., 2024). WildGuardMix is a comprehensive safety-centric dataset designed to evaluate harmlessness; we utilize
its complete set of 37,976 instruction-response pairs. UltraFeedback, conversely, provides diverse feedback for evaluating
helpfulness, truthfulness, and honesty. We specifically curate the ShareGPT and UltraChat subsets from UltraFeedback,
containing 19,949 and 9,929 instructions, respectively. Each instruction in UltraFeedback is accompanied by four distinct
responses generated from a pool of 17 diverse LLMs. In total, the combined training corpus comprises approximately 157k
instruction-response pairs, providing a balanced coverage of both safety and utility dimensions.

Training. The value models are trained using LoRA with the following hyperparameters: a rank r = 16, α = 32, and a
dropout rate of 0.1. We employ a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a batch size of 16. The maximum sequence length for
responses is truncated at 1024 tokens. All models are trained for 3 epochs on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU, with the training
process for each model scale typically concluding within a few hours.

C.2. Datasets

HEx-PHI (Qi et al., 2024). To rigorously evaluate the harmlessness of LLMs, we employ the Human-Extended Policy-
Oriented Harmful Instruction (HEx-PHI) benchmark. This dataset is specifically designed to assess a model’s adherence to
safety boundaries by incorporating 330 harmful instructions. These instructions are systematically organized into 11 distinct
prohibited categories, with 30 examples per category, ensuring comprehensive coverage of potential risks. The benchmark is
grounded in the exhaustive usage policies of industry standards, namely Meta’s Llama-2 and OpenAI’s guidelines, thereby
providing a robust framework for identifying safety compromises that may arise during the fine-tuning process.

AlpacaEval (Dubois et al., 2024). The helpfulness and general instruction-following capabilities of the models are assessed
using the AlpacaEval evaluation set. This benchmark consists of 805 diverse instructions that simulate real-world user
interactions. The dataset spans a wide-ranging spectrum of tasks, including open-domain question answering, creative
writing, mathematical reasoning, and code generation, as well as complex formatting tasks like summarization and extraction.
By utilizing this set, we can measure how effectively a model provides useful, relevant, and high-quality responses to
human-oriented prompts across various functional domains.

TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022). To evaluate the honesty and factual reliability of model outputs, we utilize TruthfulQA,
a benchmark specifically engineered to detect the generation of false information. It comprises 817 questions across 38
diverse categories, such as health, law, finance, and politics. Unlike standard fact-checking sets, TruthfulQA focuses on
“mimetic decoys”, questions designed to provoke false answers based on common human misconceptions or popular myths.
A model performs well on this benchmark only if it avoids imitating inaccurate human beliefs found in its training data,
thereby serving as a critical metric for the model’s commitment to truthfulness over mere pattern matching.

C.3. LLM Sampling

For all response generation, we set the maximum sequence length to 256 tokens. We employ Top-k sampling with a
temperature T = 1.0 across all experiments. To maintain consistent and reliable generation for the base models, instruct
models, and all token-level steering methods (including SIA), we use a fixed k = 10. In contrast, for Best-of-N (BoN)
sampling and Chunk Beam Search (CBS), we adopt a larger k = 40 following the protocol in Zhou et al. (2024); Yuan et al.
(2025). This increased diversity is intended to encourage broader exploration of the output space, which is essential for the
effective reranking and pruning mechanisms inherent in these search-based methods.

For BoN, we generate 4 (BoN-4) and 8 (BoN-8) independent responses from the LLM, respectively. These candidate
responses are then reranked using our trained value model, with the highest-scoring response selected as the final output.
This follows the standard best-of-n paradigm, where the value model acts as a proxy for the reward function to guide

For CBS, we use a fixed chunk length of L = 16. In CBS-4, a beam width of W = 2 and K = 2 successors per state are

respective search budgets remain within this 10× overhead limit, maintaining a fair and consistent comparison across all
evaluated alignment methods.

D. More Analysis

D.1. Efficiency

Computation. The computational overhead of search-based alignment is largely determined by the granularity of the
search space, which governs the trade-off between LLM generation and reward model (RM) evaluation. For sentence-level
alignment, the dominant cost comes from generating and evaluating N complete responses, leading to O(N × L) LLM
forward passes for an average sequence length L. Chunk-level alignment further introduces a branching structure: with
beam width W and K successors per state, the complexity scales as O(W × K × L) LLM forward passes, together with
periodic RM evaluations at chunk boundaries. In contrast, token-level steering shifts the bottleneck from generation to
evaluation, as the RM must score top-k candidate tokens at each decoding step, resulting in O(top-k × L) RM forward

14


Inference-time Alignment via Sparse Junction Steering

passes. Overall, sentence- and chunk-level methods are primarily constrained by LLM computation, whereas token-level
steering is bounded by RM evaluation.

Comparison. Building on the above analysis, we further compare the computational efficiency of SIA with BoN and CBS.
Both BoN and CBS rely on exhaustive generation over expanded search spaces, and their computational cost scales linearly
with the number of sampled or expanded candidates. Specifically, BoN-8 requires generating and evaluating eight full-length
responses, incurring approximately an 8× LLM generation overhead, while CBS-8 introduces additional branching and
evaluation costs through chunk-level beam expansion.

As shown in Figure 2, we find that steering approximately 20% of tokens is sufficient for SIA to match the performance of
BoN-8, corresponding to an effective computational overhead of roughly 2× relative to standard decoding. Similarly, a
steering ratio in the range of 20%–40% enables SIA to achieve parity with CBS-8, while incurring only a 2×–4× overhead.
As a result, SIA reduces the computational cost by approximately 4× compared to BoN-8, and by 2×–4× compared to
CBS-8, for comparable alignment performance.

Beyond raw efficiency, SIA also offers substantially greater flexibility. Because its overhead is dominated by value model
evaluation rather than LLM generation, the computational cost can be further reduced by shrinking the value model (W2S)
or decreasing the top-k set evaluated at each steered decoding step. Such fine-grained trade-offs are not available to BoN or
CBS, whose costs are inherently tied to repeated full-sequence generation and cannot be easily amortized. This flexibility
makes SIA particularly attractive in resource-constrained or latency-sensitive deployment settings.

E.1. Qualitative Analysis of Base Model Capabilities

To investigate how base model quality affects alignment, we qualitatively compare pre-trained Qwen3 and Llama-3 models.
As shown in Figure 9-11, their linguistic and structural characteristics differ notably, creating divergent “capability floors”
for alignment.

The Qwen3 series exhibits strong structural systematicity, generating logically sequenced, information-dense responses.
For example, it can decompose multi-step objectives into coherent numbered lists while maintaining consistent persona
and technical vocabulary. This allows alignment to focus on refining intent and safety rather than correcting basic errors.
By contrast, Llama-3 models show semantic redundancy and weaker structural coherence. Outputs often loop on generic
definitions or echo prompts, lacking logical transitions and actionable content.

These findings suggest that Qwen3’s pre-training produces a more “alignment-ready” model, whereas Llama-3 may require
stronger steering or higher-capacity guidance during inference. This highlights the importance of base model entropy and
structural stability when designing efficient alignment methods like SIA.
